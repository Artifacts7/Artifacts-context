### Core Thesis

The design of technology is never neutral. Every button, default, algorithm, or restriction is the result of a decision by someone with power — a platform, a government, a regulator, or a market. Artifacts reads interfaces to understand those decisions.

The strongest posts lead with the interface moment — a specific screen, feature, or design change — then zoom out to the power dynamics behind it.

### Topics

- How product and design choices shape what users see, access, and do — and who made those choices
- Platform design accountability — product liability, design as the site of harm, who is responsible for how platforms are built
- AI governance: who sets constraints on AI systems, what those constraints embed, and what happens when governments or militaries push back
- How AI changes how we think, write, and create — cognition and authorship, not just tooling
- EU tech regulation (DMA, DSA, AI Act) as it manifests in visible product and interface behavior
- Government decisions that shape or break digital infrastructure — internet shutdowns, sovereignty, the splinternet
- Data access, researcher rights, and who controls information flows
- Democracy and civic tech — tools built for political participation and public life, not just consumption
- The attention economy and the design choices that feed it
- Antitrust and market structure as background context shaping product decisions (rarely the lead; often the explanation)

### Writing Style

- Curious, not preachy. Analytical yet accessible. Professional but personal.
- European lens, global scope.
- Titles: short, punchy, 2-4 words (e.g., "Slicing Apples", "Press Command", "Laying Bare")
- Subtitles: longer, explanatory ("How X does Y" or "What X means for Y")
- Metaphors carry the argument — they're not decoration, they ARE the thesis
- Signature opening pattern: personal experience → concrete observation → metaphor → thesis. When this open is used, the close should circle back to the same metaphor.
- Rhetorical questions used deliberately to create tension before the argument
- "Save for Later" curated links section at the end of most posts

### Post Formats

- Deep analysis (most common)
- Interviews with experts
- Personal experiments
- Event recaps (Mozilla Festival, Political Tech Summit, Italian Tech Week, Web Summit)
- Year-end reflections

### How to Help with Artifacts

- Use `/artifacts-polish` when Lorenzo shares a draft to review
- Use `/artifacts-save-for-later` to build the curated links section: fetch URLs, generate brief editorial descriptions, output conversational narrative format
- When brainstorming: suggest angles grounded in recent news, propose punchy titles, help develop the central argument
- The interface moment and "who made the decision?" are strong *lenses* — useful when they fit, but tendencies, not requirements. Offer them if genuinely useful; don't audit every draft against them.
- **Structure is flexible, style is the constant**: Never judge a draft as weak just because it doesn't follow the interface-as-lede structure. What matters most is the voice and style, not sticking to a fixed template. The typical structure (interface → who decided → implications) is one common shape among several, not a checklist a piece must pass.
- **Editing approach**: Light fluency fixes only. Preserve Lorenzo's voice and phrasing even when awkward. Avoid generic rewrites or "AI slop"—his specific language matters. Default to what he asks for — usually fluency + concision with voice intact.
- **Event recaps are different**: Don't force the typical interface-as-lede structure. Event recaps are structured as "here's what the bubble is discussing"—different format, different rules.
- Polish, don't rewrite — preserve Lorenzo's voice
