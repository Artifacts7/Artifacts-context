As simple as that

Now, what you hear are <strong>two eerily smooth voices having a conversation.</strong> They <em>sound</em> human, even throwing in a few attitude shifts depending on the topic.

As someone who actually <em>wrote</em> the guide, I can confirm they’re hitting all the right points. Sure, <strong>they get a bit superficial at times and lean a little too heavily on certain sections</strong>, but still—it’s shockingly accurate.

Yet, what strikes me is that this AI made my dry-as-dust EU election guide sound almost… <strong>fun.</strong>

And it didn’t require a single line of code, no complicated instructions, no need for tech or AI expertise. <strong>Just drop your content in, and</strong> <em><strong>bam</strong></em><strong>, you’ve got a podcast.</strong>

------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is <strong>Google’s NotebookLM</strong>, the latest shiny AI toy from Big G to engage with any type of content and, as they claim, <em><strong>“go from information to insights”.</strong></em>

You upload your material, and it’ll spit out summaries, key points, outlines—<strong>basically anything that would’ve taken a student or intern hours.</strong>

<a href="https://notebooklm.google.com/">Give it a whirl.</a>

I remember the first time I used ChatGPT—that “wow” moment when I realized, okay, this is something. I’ve had similar moments with other AI tools over the years, and <strong>NotebookLM gave me that same feeling.</strong>

But here’s the thing: this isn’t just a “wow” moment. <strong>It’s a chance to take a step back</strong> and think about Gen AI and the hype, AI-generated content, and what it means for the future of information. And who (or what) is actually intelligent in all this?

That’s Artifacts for today—just a bit more aware that my future competition might be synthetic :)

> ### The Story

<strong>To make matters more ironic</strong>, the elections guide I uploaded was also partially AI-generated (or at least co-created) with Claude, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/posts/lorenzo-ancona-1b02b31a7_not-the-platforms-you-were-thinking-about-activity-7203289310840655872-_2kQ?utm_source=share&amp;utm_medium=member_desktop">as I mentioned back in June.</a>

So, one AI helped draft the report, and another turned it into a podcast.

<strong>Does this mean machines are getting smarter?</strong> Well, not exactly.

One key takeaway here is that <strong>there’s no real intelligence at play.</strong> Rather, intelligence is in what we perceive and in our understanding, but not in the machines.

<strong>Let me break it down.</strong> Tools like ChatGPT, NotebookLM, and others don’t possess intelligence in the traditional sense. They rely on statistics and computation to process input and produce output.

<strong>But they don’t</strong> <em><strong>understand</strong></em> <strong>the content and what they’re processing, nor do they attach any meaning or emotion to it.</strong>

<a href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4358789">As Floridi brilliantly puts it</a>, <strong>AI is “</strong><em><strong>agency without intelligence”.</strong></em> In other terms, it’s something performing actions that would require intelligence (doing a podcast) without any actual intelligence to do so.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

<em>So, where is the intelligence?</em> <strong>It’s in our perception of the text, audio, or images produced as the final output.</strong> We associate intelligence with this because, traditionally, these kinds of outcomes have required human intellect. <strong>But that’s not what’s happening in the process itself.</strong>

<a href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WxjL!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F96142294-9bd1-46b1-a706-953006151e07_360x360.gif"><img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WxjL!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F96142294-9bd1-46b1-a706-953006151e07_360x360.gif" alt=""></a>

Now, someone might ask, <em><strong>“So what? Isn’t the output what really matters?”</strong></em> Well, that’s debatable. Think of any work of art or human creation—<strong>we value not just the finished piece</strong>, but also the process, the intention, the creativity behind it.

<strong>The painter at work is just as important as the final painting.</strong>

This isn’t to dismiss AI, which clearly generates impressive outputs. But it’s key to remember that <em><strong><a href="https://direct.mit.edu/books/book/5338/Artificial-CommunicationHow-Algorithms-Produce">“What algorithms \[and AI\] are reproducing is not the intelligence of people but the informativity of communication.”</a></strong></em>

Again, AI excels at creating content bearing information value, like the podcast you heard. <strong>But it’s an artificial form of</strong> <em><strong>communication</strong></em> <strong>rather than</strong> <em><strong>intelligence.</strong></em>

We need to be mindful of this distinction so we don’t overstate what these tools are really doing.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Still, even though the processes lack intelligence, the results are increasingly indistinguishable from what humans can produce. <strong>And that’s something we have to sit with.</strong>

This shift, in my view, has two major implications:

1.  <strong>Mediocrity is at stake.</strong> AI-generated content can match the quality of low to mid-tier content, and it’s faster and cheaper to produce. But if mediocrity becomes unacceptable, how will one push the boundaries to grow toward excellence? It’s an open question.

2.  <strong>We’re facing a flood of content.</strong> Social media already boosted content creation and sharing. GenAI is about to supercharge that—making it easier, quicker, and more accessible for anyone to generate and share on-demand content.

This will create a <strong>synthetic internet</strong>, where more and more content is machine-generated, <strong>yet strikingly similar to what humans used to make.</strong>

As discouraging or even threatening as this might seem to those passionate about content creation, <strong>I still believe that human processes—intelligence, passion, and creativity—will remain valuable.</strong>

Even if, at first glance, mediocre content from a human and a machine will look almost indistinguishable. <strong>And this is already playing out, as we speak.</strong>
