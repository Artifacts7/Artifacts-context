# Use with caution
**Subtitle:** Designing the use of AI, from the debate between Anthropic and the Pentagon
**Date:** Mar 7, 2026
**URL:** https://artifactstech.substack.com/p/use-with-caution

---

In July 2025, the Pentagon awarded contracts to Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and xAI to develop frontier AI capabilities for national security. Anthropic's Claude became the only frontier AI system cleared to run on fully classified Pentagon networks.

On January 12, 2026, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth issued an AI strategy memo declaring the Pentagon would become "an 'AI-first' warfighting force with AI agents integrated 'from campaign planning to kill chain execution.'" He requested contractors remove policy constraints to allow "any lawful use."

Anthropic rejected the ultimatum on February 27, citing concerns about mass surveillance and autonomous weapons. That same day, Sam Altman announced OpenAI had reached new agreement terms with the Pentagon, claiming the same red lines were preserved—though details remained undisclosed.

The Pentagon then designated Anthropic a "Supply-Chain Risk to National Security," ordering federal agencies to cease using its products within six months. Anthropic committed to challenging this designation.

## The Core Issue: Designing Use, Not Just Technology

This conflict fundamentally concerns **constraints**—specifically, who controls them and at what level they operate.

Anthropic established two red lines:

1. **Mass domestic surveillance** is incompatible with democratic values and threatens fundamental liberties
2. **Fully autonomous weapons** require proper guardrails that currently don't exist

The Pentagon's position—that AI models should be "free from usage policy constraints"—inverts typical technology regulation debates. Usually, companies complain regulations limit capabilities; here, the government argues that technological constraints prevent lawful military applications.

## Why This Matters

With AI being general-purpose technology, the design of *how* it can be used becomes as critical as the technology itself. AI companies aren't merely building systems; they're shaping "the space of possible uses."

The absence of comprehensive federal AI regulation means "lawful use" remains undefined. This creates a governance vacuum where technological constraints—decisions embedded by builders—effectively function as policy.

The real question isn't what AI can technically accomplish, but what stakeholders will permit it to accomplish.
