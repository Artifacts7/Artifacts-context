# Out of context

**Subtitle:** From tabs to teammates: how AI is changing the browser game
**Date:** 2025-07-29

<strong>I’ve been testing what the future of browsing the internet might look like</strong> - why LLMs alone aren’t enough, and why something better, built on top of the Internet, isn’t as far-fetched as it sounds.

It’s been a fascinating journey. <strong>Here are my first takeaways.</strong>

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The big problem with large language models - and chatbots, their most popular use - is simple: <strong>they lack context.</strong>

<strong>Every time you start a task, you have to explain everything from scratch.</strong> What you’re doing, why, what you’ve already done. You end up copy-pasting fragments and <strong>repeating yourself just to get the chatbot to perform reasonably well.</strong>

A lot of the good results you might get depend less on the model and more on you - <strong>on how good you are at gathering information, giving instructions, prompting it right.</strong>

Which is why ChatGPT’s (and others) ability to (1) store long-term instructions about who you are and (2) remember things across sessions is hailed as such a big deal.

<strong>But even that’s not enough.</strong> Because what we really want is AI to <em>live inside</em> the document, slide, or code we’re working on <em>right now.</em>

Call it a companion, co-pilot, or just a helpful mate - <strong>it needs to be</strong> <em><strong>in the context</strong>,</em> not waiting on the sidelines for us to bring it up to speed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

And where does that context live, most of the time? <strong>In our browser.</strong>

Whether it’s Chrome, Safari, or something more niche, our tabs contain the bulk of what we’re doing. <strong>In many ways, the browser has become the operating system of our digital lives.</strong>

That’s why <strong>it makes so much sense for AI to be baked into our browsers</strong> - not as another tab, but <em>within</em> the flow of everything else

AI should sit <em>next to</em> whatever we’re doing. Reading what we’re reading. Seeing what we’re seeing. <strong>Getting the context</strong>, without having to be spoon-fed.

This has one immediate implication: <strong>the ChatGPT-style interface is already outdated.</strong>

What we’re moving toward is AI not as a standalone tool, but as a <em><strong>normal technology</strong></em> - integrated into the technologies we already use. <strong>The browser, this time, is front and center.</strong>

And it’s about time. Browsers haven’t changed much in the past 15 years. Sure, Chrome is slicker than Internet Explorer - but functionally, they’re not that different.

As browsers evolve into full-fledged operating systems for our online lives, <strong>AI becomes the joystick we use to play with them.</strong>

Or, to quote the genius tagline from The Browser Company: <strong>“You’ll never explain yourself again.”</strong> (Also, you’ll get amazing branzinos.)

<strong>This isn’t speculation: the industry is already headed there.</strong>

Over the past month, I’ve been testing two AI-powered browsers. One is <strong>Dia</strong>, from The Browser Company (yes, the folks behind Arc). The other is <strong>Comet</strong>, by Perplexity - the AI company Artifacts readers should already know well.

What I’ve seen is madness.

<a href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Mj7F!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5f6b3f7c-2e71-4cc2-868b-ac3ddee2c7e7_1920x1080.png"><img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Mj7F!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5f6b3f7c-2e71-4cc2-868b-ac3ddee2c7e7_1920x1080.png" alt=""></a>

Both share a powerful new feature: <strong>you can</strong> <em><strong>chat with your tabs</strong></em><strong>.</strong> And the chat sits right next to what you’re doing.

But it’s not just a UI gimmick with a dumb chatbot bolted on. <strong>These chats can</strong> <em><strong>see</strong></em> <strong>what you’re doing</strong> - so when you ask something, there’s no need to explain: “I’m writing this thing for this reason…” They already know.

Watching a YouTube video? <strong>You can chat with it.</strong>

<strong>All of this removes the usual back-and-forth with ChatGPT.</strong> No more copy-pasting. No need to pick a model - because honestly, for low-intensity tasks like drafting messages, any LLM will do.

So you just care about technology solving your problems, <strong>less about the</strong> <em><strong>how.</strong></em>

Ah, Comet doesn’t just <em>read</em> what you’re doing - <strong>it can</strong> <em><strong>take action for you:</strong></em>

What these browsers are doing is refreshingly simple: <strong>they make AI feel normal.</strong> Not magic. Not hype. Just useful.

The browser still looks like a browser - <strong>but now AI sits quietly on the side, ready when you need it.</strong> No dramatic reinvention or steep learning curve. <strong>Just a new layer of capability built into the place you already live online.</strong>

And honestly, that’s where AI belongs. <strong>In the flow of your everyday browsing,</strong> enhancing what you’re already doing - without making you start from zero every time.

<strong>No context-switching.</strong> Not for us, and not for our tools.
