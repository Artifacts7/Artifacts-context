# Please, go ahead

**Subtitle:** If AI is producing more than we can think through, and a few questions to ask
**Date:** 2026-11-25
**URL:** https://artifactstech.substack.com/p/go-ahead
**Status:** Published — captured from live article on 2026-11-25
**Archive format:** Original article text; Markdown formatting conversion. Images retain source URLs; media embeds are linked, not transcribed.

---

I've started to think that AI is sometimes **too much.**

I'm not referring to the pace of its development, [which isn't exactly slow either](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/#executive-summary), but rather to AI as a technology used in our everyday work.

And so its impact on how we think and, even before that, **on the direction of our thinking.**

What I deem too much is the **pace** at which AI spits out information but also the **volume** of information it can give back.

Sure: you can limit the usage, or tell it to answer concisely, or use it to only review things. But the premise and promise of the most advanced models from AI labs is to handle big chunks of context and processes autonomously, not just a back and forth on small tasks.

That's cause **these models power agents** that can reason and then do things, get results, reason again, and finish a task.

From my experience, an AI, in a single run, with a good prompt and pointed to the right documents and instructions, can research the flow of a lecture, draft an outline of slides, and then do the slides. Or, to broaden use cases, a good prompt on a solid codebase can bootstrap a prototype of a functioning feature within software applications (yes, to be tested, but still).

As the work on context and AI agents I once [outlined here](https://artifactstech.substack.com/p/the-artifacts-i-use-for-artifacts) has evolved significantly, I am now quite confident in delegating big chunks of work or research to virtual assistants with no need to be in every single turn of the process.

And yet, **I sometimes feel overwhelmed by the amount of information** I have to catch up with once AI agents are done. Sure, thanks to a detailed prompt the AI maybe did well, but I still may have to review 10, 15, 50 pages of text.

Another risk I see clearly is the **parallel work** these long tasks may trigger: since intellectual work may be turning into launching AI agents and waiting for them to come back, **one may be tempted to spawn different and differently complex tasks all together**, to then end up juggling too much at once.

This combination of volume and speed may trigger a dangerous thought: that **we're the bottleneck.** You may have seen that Claude or other AIs now end their answers with a sort of follow-up like *"Shall I now create a schema explaining this?"* Well, in front of the 50 pages just produced, **the temptation to just say 'yes' without reviewing what was produced earlier is not negligible.**

Or, as Claude engineering leader Addy Osmani [has put it](https://x.com/addyosmani/status/2117512471930167377), *"When you're producing more than you can stand behind, you're no longer reviewing. You're hoping."*

With, thus, **a few consequences.**

The first one: **we lose track of where our work** and, thus, cognitive effort is going and why, as we're following AI's direction.

Second, and as a consequence, **we miss the turning points, the questions, the pivots** that characterise a thinking process. When talking about this for design, Giorgia Lupi [in the NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/09/27/opinion/ai-art-creativity.html) said *"The wandering is where a lot of the actual design happens."*

If we just say *"go ahead, go ahead"*, **we don't wander, don't get lost to find a new way,** but just risk going down a road we didn't really pick.

Bonus question I've started to ask: *would I have done this task if I didn't have AI?* Besides the pace and the volume, **isn't it that we embark on more projects or side projects just cause the cost of execution is shrinking?** That's probably why [studies find](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/) that with AI we're not really working less.

It's the [Jevons paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jevons_paradox) of the modern era: similarly to how more efficient steam engines made Britain burn more coal, not less, **we do more because of AI.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

All in all, this all boils down to a risk: **we produce more because it's cheap, and because it's cheap, we may value it less.** Information is losing value and meaning.

In other words, as producing 10, 15, 50 pages of information (text, slides, images, whatever) is now almost frictionless and costless, we may just care less about it.

And so we live a sort of **inflation of meaning** in what we read and consume: the more words we print, the less each is worth.

Yet, despite production being cheaper, reading and checking are not: they still cost us effort. And so it can be dangerous: **we're tempted to produce more, but we may also lose control of the what and why of what we do**, as we're overwhelmed by it.

But then, **what's the point** of just overproducing information that is often empty of the meaning we used to assign to it?

If we stand by the assumption that [writing is thinking](https://artifactstech.substack.com/p/writing-is-thinking), **I am concerned that the pace and volume of AI hamper our thinking**, or make us lazy in it.

I'm not saying we should ban AI or stop using it, of course. But rather be careful in how much we delegate to it.

You may have seen that AI labs have recently been claiming to have solved complex math problems. In this regard, it's very interesting to read the reactions of mathematicians like Terence Tao, one of the most important living mathematicians.

In a [recent New Yorker profile](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/11/09/the-year-math-changed-forever), he argues that **new problems emerge from** ***"the slow, deliberative process"*** **of working toward a solution,** ***"and this is what's being destroyed."*** And that's problematic cause, yes, maybe answers to problems are found, but they may not be fully understood and, even worse, as the piece puts it, *"in math, answers are often not very important; the insights and inventions that arise while working toward an answer matter much more."*

So we're cracking complex things, but wandering less and losing the pure process of scratching our heads around something, which is what makes math and thinking worth it.

I'd then argue that in an age where thinking power is widely distributed and cheaper but also offloaded to machines, **we're called to train the ability to resist or, to put it more simply, to practice critical thinking.**

Which is why when it comes to using AI I've started to ask a set of questions:

- **What's the why of what I'm doing? Or better, what's the fundamental reason behind it?** And therefore, what am I trying to achieve as I have AI running this for me? David Foster Wallace, who never saw AI, said in ["This is Water"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DCbGM4mqEVw) (one of the best things I've ever read in my life) that we're really free not if we think, but **if we're capable of choosing what to think about.**

- **What do I expect out of the AI support I've just asked for?** As in, before I press 'enter' for a prompt, am I clear on what I want to get out of it? Or am I just rolling the dice?

- **Am I [cognitively surrendering](https://x.com/addyosmani/status/2117512471930167377) to AI?** It may sound catastrophic, but there's a risk of pausing thinking and not challenging what's returned. In the long run, isn't it scary?

- **Do I want to be accountable for this piece of information that has just been produced?** Recently, with Sarah, we [talked about](https://artifactstech.substack.com/p/who-takes-the-blame) how you can't offload responsibility.

- I mentioned it before, but good to reiterate: **would I be doing this if I didn't have AI?** Do I maybe have good reasons not to do this?

- Finally, as [Bill Gates wrote](https://www.gatesnotes.com/work/make-ai-work-for-everyone/reader/a-turbulent-ai-era-and-critical-choices-to-make), **have I chosen where NOT to use AI?** Maybe for my own convictions, or to keep practising something, or so as not to unlearn something, or to learn something new?

These are just some questions. **I'd be curious to know how you think about your thinking** and about the thinking of machines for you, and how you're coping with it.
