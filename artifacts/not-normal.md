# Not Normal
**Subtitle:** What the Anthropic standoff reveals about who really controls AI
**Date:** Jul 2026
**URL:** https://artifactstech.substack.com/p/not-normal

---

Artifacts is not meant to be a news newsletter, but what has happened between Anthropic and the US administration over the past few months is worth a recap. It comes in two separate episodes.

**First episode, at the start of the year.** The Pentagon wanted Anthropic to agree that its models could be used for "all lawful purposes." Anthropic refused, asking for two exceptions: no fully autonomous weapons, and no mass domestic surveillance of Americans. The administration did not take it well. It moved to label the company a "supply chain risk," and by late February the President had ordered federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology altogether. Anthropic sued; a judge called the move "classic illegal First Amendment retaliation."

**Second episode, in early summer, and a different story.** In June, Anthropic released Mythos, a model it described as too dangerous to hand over as-is, alongside Fable — the same model with built-in guardrails against malicious uses. Within days, a jailbreak was found in Fable, and reports emerged that a China-linked group had accessed Mythos. The administration reacted with export controls barring foreign nationals from using the models — including Anthropic's own non-American employees, which is odd given how many of them there are. To comply, Anthropic had to disable both models for everyone, worldwide. A few weeks later the controls were lifted, and the models are back — on the condition that Anthropic proactively detects security risks and reports malicious activity to the government.

That last condition matters. There is no formal law here yet, but a pattern is forming: the state now sets terms on when and how a model reaches the public. OpenAI, too, staggered its latest release "following pressure by the US government."

There is a clear takeaway out of the past few months: **AI is not a normal technology. Therefore, its use, deployment, and regulation cannot be normal.**

I borrow the phrase from Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, who argue the opposite in their much-cited essay *"AI as Normal Technology."* Their point is about diffusion: AI will spread and embed itself across the economy gradually, the way electricity or the internet did, and so we can govern it with the ordinary tools we already have. On that layer, they may well be right. But the past few months have played out on a different one — not the economy, but the state. And at the level of geopolitics and national strategy, none of this looks normal at all.

AI not being just a normal technology can be inferred when looking at the two main actors involved:

- AI companies (OpenAI, Anthropic etc.) who are themselves claiming to be building the future of intelligence and knowledge, as well as putting forward that some of their models are too dangerous
- The US administration, so involved in what's going on — keeping a close eye on the models and spotting, apparently, risks; but more importantly, aware that AI is a critical technology on the geopolitical realm too, with implications in the challenges with China

Let's look into this more.

For the companies, a beautiful piece from Cal Newport has criticised their approach. If they claim their models to be dangerous, it could be for two reasons. Either this is true, and therefore worrying — but then maybe the solution should be to really stop and think about what has been done? AI companies keep pushing because they know other companies will, so they can't lose ground. But if you really think what you're doing is too dangerous and you put it out there anyway, then maybe more responsibility is needed. Or maybe it's not that true, and it's just a marketing move — but is that responsible? Are we joking?

Let's assume the first is true. Last week, I had the chance to listen to Yann LeCun, former Chief AI Scientist at Meta and now founder of AMI Labs, who put it simply: if you claim it's too dangerous, what about starting to do things differently? AI technologies are far from deterministic — the people building them can put guardrails in place. So if you're concerned, just stop?

If you're aware of these risks and you're in control of the design of such technology, you draw the consequences. Otherwise, claims and warnings seem misaligned with the downstream actions. If one is to trace why, it could go up to the incentives driving these companies, which may not just be safety: they keep raising money from private investors, target IPOs with debatable financial fundamentals, and are racing against each other while being all far from profitable.

But the administration side of things is no less interesting. Even a laissez-faire administration like the current US one seems concerned about AI, and for reasons of incentive. It started by cutting any existing AI regulation framework; then things changed, with the current posture being one of vetting.

What they show, beyond regulation, is that the US administration **needs** AI — and thus has incentives around it — for a couple of reasons: it is a critical technology for war operations (and the world is not peaceful right now), but it is also a matter of competitiveness on the global field, where the threat coming from China is not irrelevant.

And therefore the grip on AI is different from previous attempts at regulating technologies like online platforms or data privacy. It pertains more to a strategic technology.

It is not dissimilar from nuclear technology, for which special plans were put together (you watched *Oppenheimer*, no?). With, yet, the small detail that this strategic technology is not being developed within universities or public settings, but by private organisations that, of course, are looking to be profitable.

So, reconciling the two positions, we are facing a more-than-normal technology, with alarms and warnings about safety raised by those building it and those regulating it, but with all of it pulled by other incentives too.

I once read that incentives are superpowers (from someone who knows one or two things about AI). Here they seem to be many, and misaligned — within and across AI companies and the administration — while all acknowledge (or even raise!) the potential dangers or threats.

If these dangers or threats are real, lining up incentives is not enough; they have to be prioritised. Here is a test for whether that is happening. When a company says a model is too dangerous, it is describing an obligation it now carries, and we should hold it to that. Too dangerous to release, released anyway, banned abroad, back by morning: as long as those words cost nothing, they are just another way to launch a product.

We built institutions around the last strategic technology. This one we have handed to companies racing each other to an IPO, and left them to sort out the contradictions in public. Meanwhile the settlement between private incentive and public stake still has not been made, and it is users and citizens who pay for the delay.
