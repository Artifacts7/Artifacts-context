Or at least, <strong>if AI really is the future of tools, it probably won’t arrive wearing a chatbot mask</strong> - the one that took the world by storm just a few years ago.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let’s think through this.

<strong>Take Word, for instance.</strong> When was the last time you started a document without at least checking in with an AI for ideas, a structure, or a template? And if you <em>did</em> start solo, how long before you copied your rough draft into ChatGPT (or your LLM of choice) to get feedback, edit suggestions, or just a nudge?

<strong>So the least we can expect is to have that AI companion</strong> <em><strong>already there</strong></em>. Present not just in spirit or on another tab, but right inside the interface itself.

<strong>Now look at chatbots.</strong> The generic ones, the tab-switching kind, that require instructions for every new project. The ones that don’t know what you’re working on, what you’ve written before, or what your app even <em>does</em>. <strong>That’s not a sleek user experience.</strong>

<img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!85mj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_lossy/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff59ee897-8882-41ad-b89b-646685f42a60_220x146.gif" alt="Bad Computer GIFs | Tenor" title="Bad Computer GIFs | Tenor">

when you start the back and forth with chatbots

Word is <em>just</em> an example of this. It’s a much broader matter: <strong>how AI may shape and change interfaces.</strong>

Or, put differently, <strong>how the AI revolution may be, before anything else, an interface revolution.</strong>

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let’s pivot to a different example.

<strong>I hate pickles.</strong> I also hate figuring out how to remove them from burgers in food delivery apps.

Sometimes the customization options appear upfront. Sometimes they’re buried before checkout. Sometimes it’s a list of toggles. Sometimes it’s a freeform text box. Every app a new quest.

<strong>What if I could just</strong> <em><strong>say</strong></em> <strong>what I want?</strong> Like: <em>“No pickles. Make it rare. Also, extra crispy fries.”</em>

Natural language. No hunting around in weird menus. No decoding the logic of some designer’s idea of “flow.”

Overall, <strong>without</strong> <em><strong>discovering</strong></em> <strong>and</strong> <em><strong>understanding</strong></em> <strong>the app</strong> but just by <em>typing</em> in my own words.

In this case, <strong>AI isn’t the interface itself (like in a chatbot).</strong> <strong>It’s the medium through which I interact with the app.</strong>

<strong>I don’t need to go and click around to understand how to customise my order.</strong> And the same applies to, say, interpreting a complex data set I’ve been given to analyse or a multi-step process I need to set up for the sales strategy of my company.

<strong>Instead of translating my intentions into clicks, I use my words</strong> — and the AI translates them into actions. It’s a <strong>different paradigm</strong>.

This shift means interfaces can become <strong>simpler, less crowded</strong>, and more intuitive. <strong>We don’t need to understand</strong> <em><strong>how</strong></em> <strong>they work.</strong> We just need them to work <em>with</em> us.

<strong>Because not everyone necessarily cares about an interface.</strong> We care more about what it lets us do.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

You might argue: <em>“Okay, but aren’t these just AI agents? Nothing (too) new.”</em>

Not quite. <strong>Agents are the next level</strong> - the background operators that respond to context, act autonomously, send emails, book meetings. They’re backstage.

<strong>What we’re talking about here is the stage itself.</strong> The front-of-house experience. The AI-as-mediator, the one that <em>sits between you and the interface</em>, reshaping how you get things done.

<strong>In this framing, AI isn’t about unlocking new capabilities.</strong> It’s about <strong>reframing old ones</strong> — taking the exact same workflows (ordering food, writing a doc, configuring sales tools) and making them smoother.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

<strong>All of this has some consequences.</strong>

Using plain language to interact with technological artifacts <strong>should be easier</strong>, and could make our lives easier.

<strong>This could mean screens that don’t just</strong> <em><strong>display</strong></em> <strong>things, but respond.</strong> Nudge. Adapt.

Like Netflix suggestions - which, let’s face it, were doing AI before it was cool. <strong>We could get screens that adapt to our needs</strong> and try accompany us towards what is inferred to be our desired outcome.

Start a doc on the history of politics? It offers a timeline template. Launch a sales plan? It reminds you of last quarter’s targets.

Of course, <strong>there are risks.</strong>

<strong>Where’s the line of agency?</strong> What does the AI get to do without your explicit approval? How do you <em>know</em> what it’s doing? Can you override it? <strong>Can you understand</strong> <em><strong>why</strong></em> <strong>it made the choices it made?</strong>

<strong>Not only: what control do we have over</strong> <em><strong>processes</strong></em> <strong>themselves?</strong> If the AI I’m using to co-write a document gets to weird conclusions, how can I see and steer it? Explainability is a thing, and <a href="https://artifactstech.substack.com/p/breaking-the-chains">has already been in Artifacts !</a>

<strong>More importantly, what about failure?</strong> What if the AI can’t get rid of the pickles? Do I still have a backup way to manually fix it? <strong>Will there still be buttons for when things go sideways?</strong>

<a href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yOrL!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F19c2e0e0-0265-45e6-aa8e-8295a84de213_1024x1024.png"><img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yOrL!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F19c2e0e0-0265-45e6-aa8e-8295a84de213_1024x1024.png" alt=""></a>

no please no!

And, yes, <strong>eventually voice</strong> <em><strong>might</strong></em> <strong>be the final form of this interaction,</strong> instead of text<strong>.</strong> But do we really want to work in offices where everyone is talking out loud to their computer like they’re casting spells? <em>There’ll be time to look into this in future Artifacts.</em>

------------------------------------------------------------------------

But let’s not go to some sci-fi singularity.

We may not become radically more efficient beings, <strong>but we’re likely to be</strong> <em><strong>doing things differently</strong></em><strong>.</strong>

Because the way we interface with tools is adapting to a new kind of medium - one that can understand us more directly.

It’s not the chatbot or AI that matters.

<strong>It’s what happens when the interface starts speaking our language,</strong> thanks to what is in between and mediates the relationship between us and artifacts.
