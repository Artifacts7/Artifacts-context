# You know a good architect?
**Subtitle:** So to go fix a few things that may be wrong with the design of social media platforms

A museum visit in Amsterdam prompted a reflection on design's impact. Despite unchanged artwork, "the experience was notably worse" after restructuring—new layout, different lighting, broken visitor flow. This observation parallels recent social media legal developments: "nothing had changed about the art but everything had changed about the building around it."

## Two Landmark Verdicts

Recent court rulings marked a significant shift in platform accountability:

1. **Los Angeles Case (March 25)**: A jury found Meta and YouTube negligent in platform design, specifically targeting "infinite scroll, autoplay, and algorithmically-tuned reward loops" as foreseeable harm sources rather than neutral choices. The plaintiff sued after spending up to 16 hours daily on Instagram as a teenager.

2. **New Mexico Case (March 24)**: Meta faced a $375 million judgment after an undercover investigation proved the company "had misled users about how safe its platforms were for children" and enabled predator access to minors.

## The Section 230 Framework

Historically, platforms benefited from Section 230 protections—"the 26 words that created the Internet"—shielding them from liability for user-generated content. However, platforms evolved beyond 1996's "bulletin board" model. Today's systems profile users, personalize feeds, recommend content, and shape cultural visibility algorithmically.

## Product Liability as Solution

These cases introduced product liability doctrine to digital platforms, examining both defects of commission (harmful features) and omission (missing protections). This requires legal foundations currently absent:

- Clear definition of digital "products"
- Design-defect standards for software
- Causation doctrine for systemic harm
- First Amendment boundaries separating design from editorial expression

## Global Regulatory Parallels

The EU's Digital Services Act approaches similar concerns through different mechanisms. In December, the Commission fined X €120 million partly for "blue checkmark" design choices. Investigations into Snapchat followed, focusing on age verification and stranger exposure defaults.
