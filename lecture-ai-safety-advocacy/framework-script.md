# The Four Pillars — a lecture script

*A read-aloud walkthrough of the WHY framework, for a global-advocacy audience. Spoken passages are in plain text; **[CUE]** lines tell you which reading to lean on and what to put on the slide. Companion to `framework-reading-list.md`. Sequence: Motivation → Legitimation → Instrument → (segue to the HOW).*

---

## Cold open — the paradox

Start with a puzzle.

For most of the history of capitalism, industries have fought *against* regulation. Tobacco, oil, finance, big tech in its social-media era — the standard corporate posture is to lobby to be left alone. Yet from 2015 onwards, the most powerful artificial-intelligence companies in the world have been doing the opposite. Their CEOs go to the Senate and *ask* to be regulated. They publish open letters demanding governments act. They warn that their own product could end the human race.

So — the question for today. Why would an industry advocate for the regulation of *itself*? And — because this is a course on advocacy — *how* do they do it, and what does that tell us about power?

My argument is that this is not a puzzle about corporate altruism. It is a case study in advocacy. These labs are not reacting to policy — they are *authoring* it. And to see how, we need four lenses. Three of them explain the WHY. The fourth hands us the tools to read the HOW.

## Pillar 1 — Advocacy & Norms

Start here — this pillar reframes the rest.

**[CUE — lead reading: Finnemore & Sikkink (1998), "International Norm Dynamics and Political Change."]**

Finnemore and Sikkink give us the **norm entrepreneur** and the **norm life cycle**: norms *emerge*, then *cascade* as more actors adopt them, then get *internalized* until they feel like common sense. The point that matters for us is that norms have *authors*. Someone stands at the beginning of the cycle and says: "this is the thing we should all care about, and this is what caring about it looks like."

One precision, so we're honest about what we're doing. Finnemore and Sikkink built this framework to describe *principled* actors — human-rights campaigners, the movement to ban landmines, the Red Cross. Not corporations. So I'm not telling you they wrote about AI companies; they didn't. What I'm doing is taking their framework and applying it to what is happening now — and what these labs are doing looks a great deal like norm entrepreneurship.

Now look at what the AI labs did. They arrived early — before there was any regulation, before most governments understood the technology — and they planted a norm: **"AI must be governed."** That sounds responsible. But watch what comes bundled with it. To be the entrepreneur of a norm is to define its *content*. And the labs didn't just say AI must be governed — they told us **what the risk actually is.**

Two more readings sharpen it.

**[CUE — Haas (1992), "Epistemic Communities and International Policy Coordination."]**

Peter Haas explains why, under deep uncertainty, small networks of recognized experts get to define both the problem and the menu of solutions. Nobody in a Senate committee can independently assess whether a large language model poses an extinction risk. So the people who *build* the models become the people who *define the danger*. Expertise becomes authority. Authority becomes agenda control.

**[CUE — Bachrach & Baratz (1962), "Two Faces of Power." Optional deepen with Lukes (2005).]**

The last move is the quietest one. Bachrach and Baratz describe the "second face" of power: not winning the argument, but deciding *which arguments reach the table at all* — the power of the **non-decision**. When the labs foreground existential risk — superintelligence, loss of control, extinction — they are also, quietly, deciding what we *won't* be talking about: labour displacement, copyright, surveillance, market concentration, the environmental cost of compute. Those harms don't get argued and lost. They simply never become the agenda.

So Pillar 1's answer to WHY is this: **calling for regulation is itself an act of advocacy and agenda-setting.** The labs are norm entrepreneurs who, by defining what AI risk *is*, get to shape what any future regulation will be *about*. They are inside the norm, shaping it, from the start.

**[Transition:] But norms alone don't explain the intensity of the effort. For that, follow the money.]**

## Pillar 2 — Regulatory Politics & Capture

If Pillar 1 is about ideas, Pillar 2 is about interest. And it is the more uncomfortable half of the answer.

**[CUE — lead reading: Stigler (1971), "The Theory of Economic Regulation."]**

George Stigler's argument is blunt: regulation is often not imposed *on* an industry against its will — it is, in his words, "acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit." Regulation, in other words, can be a resource. The regulated capture the regulator, and the rules end up protecting the incumbents.

Now apply Stigler's hypothesis as a *test* to what we're seeing. When a leading lab asks to be regulated, ask Stigler's question: is this a public-interest act, or is it an attempt to **shape the regulation that is coming anyway** — to make sure that when the rules arrive, they are the labs' rules?

Because regulation is not just a constraint. It is a competitive instrument. The point of wanting a *strong say* in the incoming regime is that the terms you help write become the terms your rivals must live under. Compliance costs, licensing regimes, safety-testing mandates — these are trivial for a company with billions in capital and hundreds of staff, and potentially fatal for a startup or an open-source project. The regulation that a big lab designs can raise the drawbridge behind it.

So Pillar 2's answer to WHY is: **the labs advocate for regulation because they have a strong strategic interest in authoring it.** Getting there first, and shaping the rule, is a way of locking in advantage under the cover of responsibility.

**[Transition:] Which raises a problem for them. If the motive is partly strategic, how do you make it *look* like principle? That is Pillar 3.]**

## Pillar 3 — Legitimacy & Symbolic Politics

Pillar 3 explains the *packaging* — why the advocacy takes the specific form of ethics, safety, and voluntary commitment.

**[CUE — lead reading: Wagner (2018), "Ethics as an Escape from Regulation: From Ethics-Washing to Ethics-Shopping?"]**

Ben Wagner examines how firms use the language of *ethics* and *responsibility* as a mode of self-governance. When a company publishes principles, sets up a safety board, signs a voluntary commitment, it is demonstrating responsibility — showing that it is already acting on the problem, before anyone has told it to. And while a legislature is still writing the rules, that self-authored responsibility is what fills the space where binding law does not yet exist.

The timing is what makes this a global-advocacy story. Democracies are slow. Legislatures deliberate; the EU takes years; the US Congress stalls. Into that lag steps the industry, saying: *we take this seriously, look, we are already governing ourselves.* By behaving in a way that reads as "ethical," and by wanting to be *perceived* as ethical, the companies get to **set, themselves, the constraints they place on their own technology** — before the state can. Voluntary principles fill the space until law arrives, and help shape what it looks like when it does.

And here the *interest* comes into view — not as hypocrisy, but as the ordinary logic of advocacy. When a firm authors its own standard, it authors one it can live with. It becomes both the regulated and the author of the rule by which it is judged. That is simply what advocacy is: pursuing your interest by shaping the terms of the debate. The labs are not hiding this; they are doing it in the open, and doing it well.

So Pillar 3's answer to WHY is: **advocacy-for-regulation is also a way of building legitimacy.** You demonstrate responsibility while the rules take shape, so that when they arrive they are rules you already fit. You fill the democratic lag with your own voluntary order.

**[Transition:] Now we have the WHY — motivation, interest, legitimation. The last pillar is different. It doesn't add a new motive. It hands us the tools to see how all of this is *delivered*. Because it is delivered as a story.]**

## Pillar 4 — Narrative & Framing (the bridge to the HOW)

Everything so far — norm entrepreneurship, strategic interest, legitimation — reaches the public and the policymaker through one medium: **narrative.**

**[CUE — lead reading: Jones & McBeth (2010), "A Narrative Policy Framework."]**

The Narrative Policy Framework makes a strong claim: policy narratives are not decoration on top of politics — they are a *variable* that shapes outcomes, and they can be studied empirically. Every policy narrative, whatever the issue, has the same skeleton: a **setting**, a cast of **characters** (victims, villains, heroes), a **plot**, and a **moral** — and in the NPF, the moral of the story *is* the policy solution being proposed.

We'll use that skeleton in the second half of the course to dissect the labs' actual announcements. For now, notice the shape it reveals.

Run the AI-safety story through the grid:
- the **villain** is the technology itself — rogue, uncontrolled AI — or "reckless actors," almost never the labs' own incentives;
- the **victim** is *humanity at scale* — extinction, not the specific people harmed today;
- the **hero** is the responsible lab, working hand-in-hand with a wise regulator;
- and the **moral** — the policy solution — is, conveniently, the regime the teller is best positioned to satisfy.

**[CUE — anchor reading: Coldicutt (2024), "AI Safety Is a Narrative Problem."]**

This is what Coldicutt means when she reads AI safety as a *narrative problem*: the existential-risk story borrows the grammar of myth and tragedy, and it casts the technologists as figures of almost god-like consequence. The story doesn't just describe the labs — it *positions* them.

That positioning is where the four pillars meet. Across all of them, the labs converge on a single self-portrait: **they are not merely contributors to regulation — they are the ones uniquely fit to author it, because they are the ones who understand the danger, and, in the largest version of the story, the ones who will save the world from it.** The norm entrepreneur, the strategic incumbent, and the ethical self-governor all resolve into the same character: the indispensable hero.

**[Transition to the HOW:] So that is the WHY. Norms and agenda-setting; strategic interest in capture; legitimation through ethics; all delivered as a narrative that makes the labs the hero of their own regulation. In the next session, we test it. We take sixteen real announcements — from OpenAI's founding to Amodei's essays — and we run them through the Narrative Policy Framework, and we ask, source by source: who is the hero, who is the villain, who is the victim, and whose interests does the moral of the story actually serve?]**
