The paradox

An industry that asks to be regulated

  • Industries usually fight regulation — tobacco, oil, finance, social media.
  • Since 2015 the top AI labs do the opposite: they ask to be regulated.
  • They warn that their own product could end humanity.
  • Why would an industry advocate to regulate itself — and what does that reveal about power?

Pillar 1Why advocate at all

Pillar 1 · Advocacy & Norms

Finnemore & Sikkink (1998) — norm entrepreneurs
  • The labs arrive early and plant a norm: “AI must be governed.”
  • To author a norm is to define its content — so they also define what the risk is.
Haas (1992) — epistemic communities
  • Under uncertainty, the experts who build the models get to define the danger.
Bachrach & Baratz (1962) — the non-decision
  • Foregrounding extinction pushes present harms — labour, copyright, surveillance — off the agenda.

In one line

Defining the risk is setting the agenda.

Pillar 2Why want a strong say

Pillar 2 · Regulatory Politics & Capture

Stigler (1971) — the theory of economic regulation
  • Regulation is often “acquired by the industry… operated primarily for its benefit.”
  • The terms you help write become the terms your rivals must live under.
Salop & Scheffman (1983) — raising rivals' costs
  • Licensing and compliance are trivial for incumbents, potentially fatal for startups and open-source.
  • Asking to be regulated can raise the drawbridge behind you.

In one line

Authoring the rule is a competitive move.

Pillar 3Why frame it as ethics

Pillar 3 · Legitimacy & Symbolic Politics

Wagner (2018) — ethics as self-governance
  • While legislatures are slow, voluntary ethics and safety commitments fill the gap.
  • Performing responsibility earns legitimacy — and pre-empts harder external rules.
Edelman et al. (1999) — legal endogeneity
  • A firm that authors its own standard authors one it can live with.
  • Those voluntary frameworks can become the de facto legal standard.

In one line

Fill democracy's lag — and shape the law that follows.

Pillar 4 · Narrative & Framing

Jones & McBeth (2010) — the Narrative Policy Framework
  • Every motive above reaches the public through one medium: narrative.
  • A policy narrative has a cast — victim, villain, hero — and a moral that is the policy it wants.
Coldicutt (2024) — “AI safety is a narrative problem”
  • The existential-risk story positions the labs as the indispensable hero.

In one line

The story casts the lab as the indispensable hero.

Jones & McBeth (2010) · the toolStep 1 — the empty framework

The Narrative Policy Framework

Setting
The facts taken as given — the backdrop nobody argues with.
Victim
Who is harmed.
Villain
Who causes the harm.
Hero
Who promises the fix.
Plot
The arc that links them — decline, rescue, an out-of-control race.
Moral
The fix on offer — and in the NPF, the moral is the proposed policy.
Devil-shift
Exaggerate the villain's power — make the threat maximal.
Angel-shift
Exaggerate the hero's virtue — the one actor who can fix it.
