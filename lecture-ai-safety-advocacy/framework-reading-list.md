# Why AI Labs Ask to Be Regulated — A Theoretical Framework & Annotated Reading List

**Lecture question:** Why do AI labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google) advocate for regulation of their own industry, and what narrative do they use?

**Audience:** Political science students in a course on **global advocacy**. The framework therefore foregrounds advocacy and political-science theory (norms, agenda-setting, epistemic communities, regulatory politics, framing) and treats the labs' *rhetoric* (the HOW) as the final step, downstream of the *dynamics* (the WHY).

**How to read this list:** Pillars 1–3 build the WHY — the motivational and strategic account of why a firm would call for its own regulation. Pillar 4 bridges to the HOW by giving you the tools to dissect the specific narrative ("AI safety," "existential risk," "responsible scaling") the labs deploy. Every citation below was checked against its venue/publisher for author, year, and title.

**Verification status:** 24 sources, all verified to exist with correct author/year/venue. 23 are peer-reviewed articles or books from academic presses; 1 (Vinsel) is a widely-cited non-peer-reviewed essay, flagged as such. 0 unverified. No source is included that could not be confirmed.

## Pillar 1 — Advocacy & Norms (FOREGROUND)

*What this pillar contributes to the WHY:* This is the spine for a global-advocacy course. It reframes the labs not as firms reacting to policy but as **political actors trying to author the norms and the agenda of a new governance field**. Norm-life-cycle and transnational-advocacy theory explain why a first-mover would want to *seed* a norm ("AI must be governed") early and be its entrepreneur; epistemic-community theory explains why technical experts get to define the problem under uncertainty; the power/agenda-setting classics explain the deepest move of all — controlling which questions ever reach the table. Together they answer: calling for regulation is itself an act of advocacy and agenda-setting, not surrender to it.

1. **Finnemore, Martha, & Kathryn Sikkink (1998). "International Norm Dynamics and Political Change." *International Organization* 52(4): 887–917.**
   The canonical "norm life cycle" (emergence → cascade → internalization) and the concept of the *norm entrepreneur*. Built to describe *principled* actors — activists, humanitarian campaigners, the Red Cross — not corporations. — *Advocacy angle:* Use it as an applied lens, not a claim that Finnemore & Sikkink wrote about firms: read the labs as behaving like norm entrepreneurs, arriving early to define "responsible AI" before the norm settles on someone else's terms.

2. **Keck, Margaret E., & Kathryn Sikkink (1998). *Activists Beyond Borders: Advocacy Networks in International Politics*. Ithaca, NY: Cornell University Press.**
   Transnational advocacy networks (TANs) mobilize information, symbols, and leverage across borders to shift state behavior. — *Advocacy angle:* The labs, safety institutes, and allied NGOs behave like a TAN; use it to ask who is inside the network and whose leverage the "AI safety" frame actually amplifies.

3. **Haas, Peter M. (1992). "Introduction: Epistemic Communities and International Policy Coordination." *International Organization* 46(1): 1–35.**
   Under uncertainty, networks of recognized experts capture the definition of a problem and the menu of solutions. — *Advocacy angle:* Explains *why lab scientists* — not legislators — get to frame AI risk; control over knowledge is a form of power, the central mechanism behind lab-led regulatory proposals.

4. **Bachrach, Peter, & Morton S. Baratz (1962). "Two Faces of Power." *American Political Science Review* 56(4): 947–952.**
   The "second face" of power: agenda-setting and *non-decisions* — keeping some issues off the table entirely. — *Advocacy angle:* The sharpest lens on selective advocacy — labs foreground existential risk while non-deciding present-day harms, labor, copyright, and market concentration.

5. **Lukes, Steven (2005). *Power: A Radical View* (2nd ed.). Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan. (Orig. 1974.)**
   Adds the "third face": shaping preferences and perceptions so that conflict never arises. — *Advocacy angle:* Explains the deepest WHY — advocacy that makes the public and regulators *want* what the labs want, accepting the industry's framing of what AI even is and which risks matter.

6. **Tallberg, Jonas, Eva Erman, Markus Furendal, Johannes Geith, Mark Klamberg, & Magnus Lundgren (2023). "The Global Governance of Artificial Intelligence: Next Steps for Empirical and Normative Research." *International Studies Review* 25(3): viad040.**
   State-of-the-art IR survey of who is building AI governance and how private actors shape it. — *Advocacy angle:* The bridge that applies Pillar-1 theory directly to AI; use it to locate the labs within the emerging governance regime complex.

## Pillar 2 — Regulatory Politics & Capture (the economic/strategic WHY)

*What this pillar contributes to the WHY:* Where Pillar 1 is about norms and ideas, this pillar supplies the **material, strategic motive**. Regulation is not only a constraint — it is a competitive instrument. Capture theory shows regulation tends to serve incumbents; "raising rivals' costs" shows how compliance burdens can entrench leaders and lock out challengers; responsive-regulation theory explains why regulators invite the regulated to co-design rules (and how that access is captured); regulatory-entrepreneurship theory shows firms that treat *changing the law* as part of the business plan. This is the cynical-but-rigorous half of the answer: asking to be regulated can be a moat-building move.

1. **Stigler, George J. (1971). "The Theory of Economic Regulation." *The Bell Journal of Economics and Management Science* 2(1): 3–21.**
   Foundational capture theory: regulation is often "acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit." — *Advocacy angle:* The base hypothesis to test against lab advocacy — is a call for regulation a public-interest act or a capture strategy?

2. **Salop, Steven C., & David T. Scheffman (1983). "Raising Rivals' Costs." *American Economic Review* 73(2): 267–271.**
   A dominant firm can profit by raising competitors' costs rather than lowering its own prices. — *Advocacy angle:* The mechanism by which licensing, audit, and compute-reporting mandates favored by leading labs can burden open-source and smaller entrants — regulation as competitive weapon.

3. **Ayres, Ian, & John Braithwaite (1992). *Responsive Regulation: Transcending the Deregulation Debate*. New York: Oxford University Press.**
   The "regulatory pyramid" and enforced self-regulation — regulators and firms co-produce rules, escalating only as needed. — *Advocacy angle:* Explains *why regulators welcome* lab participation, and where that collaborative design shades into capture and privileged access.

4. **Pollman, Elizabeth, & Jordan M. Barry (2017). "Regulatory Entrepreneurship." *Southern California Law Review* 90(3): 383–448.**
   Firms (Uber, Airbnb, Tesla) whose business plan includes actively changing the law — operating in gray zones, growing "too big to ban," mobilizing users. — *Advocacy angle:* Directly names the behavior; reframes lab lobbying as entrepreneurship *over the rules themselves*, a strategic act rather than compliance.

5. **Clark, Jack, & Gillian K. Hadfield (2019). "Regulatory Markets for AI Safety." arXiv:2001.00078.**
   Proposes private, competing regulators licensed by governments to oversee AI — co-authored by an (then-)OpenAI figure and a leading law-and-economics scholar. — *Advocacy angle:* A primary artifact of lab-authored governance design; read it *as advocacy* to see the labs proposing the very market in which they would be regulated. (See also Hadfield & Clark's 2023 elaboration, "Regulatory Markets: The Future of AI Governance," arXiv:2304.04914.)

6. **Cihon, Peter, Matthijs M. Maas, & Luke Kemp (2020). "Should Artificial Intelligence Governance Be Centralised? Design Lessons from History." *Proceedings of the 2020 AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society* (AIES '20).**
   Institutional-design analysis of centralized vs. fragmented AI governance. — *Advocacy angle:* Use it to interrogate *which institutional forms* lab advocacy pushes (central bodies, licensing, compute thresholds) and whose strategic interests those forms serve — the "compute governance as moat" question.

## Pillar 3 — Legitimacy & Symbolic Politics (WHY: preempt & legitimate)

*What this pillar contributes to the WHY:* This pillar explains regulation-advocacy as a **legitimacy and preemption strategy**: performing responsibility to forestall binding external rules, defuse criticism, and secure a social license to keep building. "Ethics washing" and legal-endogeneity theory show how firms substitute symbolic self-governance (boards, principles, voluntary commitments) for enforceable constraints — and how courts and regulators then treat these gestures as if they were compliance. Calling for regulation can be the most sophisticated version of this move: you look like the adult in the room while shaping the rule you'll be measured against.

1. **Bietti, Elettra (2020). "From Ethics Washing to Ethics Bashing: A View on Tech Ethics from Within Moral Philosophy." *Proceedings of the 2020 Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency* (FAT* '20): 210–219.**
   Dissects how "ethics" is weaponized to justify self-regulation and forestall binding law. — *Advocacy angle:* Core diagnosis of the preemption move; equips students to distinguish genuine normative argument from strategic ethics-talk.

2. **Wagner, Ben (2018). "Ethics as an Escape from Regulation: From Ethics-Washing to Ethics-Shopping?" In M. Hildebrandt & K. O'Hara (eds.), *Being Profiled: Cogitas Ergo Sum*. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 84–89.**
   Examines how firms deploy the language of ethics and responsibility as self-governance (and coins "ethics-shopping"). — *Advocacy angle (as used here):* read constructively rather than as pure evasion — while binding law is still being written, voluntary principles and responsibility claims let labs act proactively and help shape the rules that eventually arrive.

3. **Green, Ben (2021). "The Contestation of Tech Ethics: A Sociotechnical Approach to Technology Ethics in Practice." *Journal of Social Computing* 2(3): 209–225.**
   Tech ethics is "vague and toothless," subsumed into corporate logics, enabling ethics-washing that resists government regulation. — *Advocacy angle:* Argues ethics is a *site of contestation over power*; asks who defines "responsible AI" and to what effect.

4. **Edelman, Lauren B., Christopher Uggen, & Howard S. Erlanger (1999). "The Endogeneity of Legal Regulation: Grievance Procedures as Rational Myth." *American Journal of Sociology* 105(2): 406–454.**
   Legal-endogeneity theory: organizations create symbolic compliance structures that courts then accept as *evidence* of compliance — firms end up shaping the law's own meaning. — *Advocacy angle:* The deep mechanism behind self-regulation-as-preemption — how voluntary safety frameworks can become the de facto legal standard the industry authored.

## Pillar 4 — Narrative & Framing / Sociology of Expectations (the bridge to the HOW)

*What this pillar contributes to the WHY→HOW bridge:* Having established *why* labs advocate, this pillar hands students the analytic tools to name and dissect *the specific narrative* they use — "AI safety," "p(doom)," "existential risk," "responsible scaling." Framing and Narrative-Policy-Framework theory show how a problem definition is constructed and made politically potent; the sociology of expectations shows how promissory visions of a technology's future do real present-day work (attracting capital, structuring regulation) before the technology exists; and the critical AI-discourse literature applies all of this to existential-risk talk specifically. This is also where the anchor article sits.

**Anchor article — situate here:**
**Coldicutt, Rachel (2024). "AI Safety Is a Narrative Problem." *Harvard Data Science Review*, Special Issue 5 (MIT Press).**
Uses literary-critical technique to read the 2023 "AI safety hype roadshow," arguing that p(doom)/existential-risk discourse is media-friendly *narrative* that crowds out present harms. — *Placement:* Belongs squarely in Pillar 4. It is the lecturer's hinge from WHY to HOW: the labs' regulation-advocacy is delivered *as a story*, and the story is the instrument. Pair it with Vinsel and Gebru & Torres below.

1. **Jones, Michael D., & Mark K. McBeth (2010). "A Narrative Policy Framework: Clear Enough to Be Wrong?" *Policy Studies Journal* 38(2): 329–353.**
   Establishes the Narrative Policy Framework (NPF) — policy narratives (setting, characters, plot, moral) can be studied empirically. — *Advocacy angle:* The rigorous toolkit for coding the "AI safety" narrative's heroes, villains, and victims. (Companion: Shanahan, Jones, McBeth & Radaelli (2017), "The Narrative Policy Framework," in Weible & Sabatier, eds., *Theories of the Policy Process*, 4th ed., 173–213.)

2. **Benford, Robert D., & David A. Snow (2000). "Framing Processes and Social Movements: An Overview and Assessment." *Annual Review of Sociology* 26: 611–639.**
   Collective action frames — diagnostic, prognostic, motivational — and frame resonance. — *Advocacy angle:* Shows how "we must be regulated to be safe" is built as a mobilizing frame, and why it resonates.

3. **Entman, Robert M. (1993). "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm." *Journal of Communication* 43(4): 51–58.**
   The classic definition: to frame is to select and make salient a particular problem definition, causal story, and remedy. — *Advocacy angle:* Explains how foregrounding existential risk *selects* one problem definition and its preferred remedy (lab-friendly regulation) while backgrounding others.

4. **Borup, Mads, Nik Brown, Kornelia Konrad, & Harro van Lente (2006). "The Sociology of Expectations in Science and Technology." *Technology Analysis & Strategic Management* 18(3–4): 285–298.**
   Expectations and promissory visions of a technology's future are performative — they attract resources and structure governance before the technology arrives. — *Advocacy angle:* Explains why hyping future superintelligence is strategically useful: the *promise* of world-changing (and world-ending) AI justifies present-day regulatory positioning.

5. **Vinsel, Lee (2021). "You're Doing It Wrong: Notes on Criticism and Technology Hype." Essay (originally Medium; archived at Hype Studies). [Non-peer-reviewed, widely cited.]**
   Coins "criti-hype": criticism that amplifies a technology's imagined power by inverting the boosters' hype into catastrophe. — *Advocacy angle:* Names the mechanism by which existential-risk *warnings* end up marketing the technology's supposed power — helping explain why labs benefit from their own doom talk.

6. **Gebru, Timnit, & Émile P. Torres (2024). "The TESCREAL Bundle: Eugenics and the Promise of Utopia Through Artificial General Intelligence." *First Monday* 29(4).**
   Traces the ideological bundle (transhumanism, longtermism, effective altruism, etc.) underpinning AGI utopianism/doomerism and its eugenic lineage. — *Advocacy angle:* A genealogy of the *worldview* that makes existential-risk framing intelligible and authoritative — essential for critically situating who benefits from the narrative.

7. **Hoes, Emma, & Fabrizio Gilardi (2025). "Existential Risk Narratives About AI Do Not Distract From Its Immediate Harms." *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 122 (preregistered survey experiments, N = 10,800).**
   Empirically tests — and partly complicates — the popular "existential risk is a distraction" thesis. — *Advocacy angle:* Keeps the framework honest: include it so students weigh the strategic-distraction reading against experimental evidence rather than taking the critique on faith.

## Suggested Core Spine (minimal set for a global-advocacy audience)

For a single lecture that builds the WHY before the HOW, assign these seven:

1. **Finnemore & Sikkink (1998)** — the norm-entrepreneur template: labs as authors of a new norm.
2. **Bachrach & Baratz (1962)** *or* **Lukes (2005)** — the agenda-setting / non-decision lens: what selective advocacy leaves off the table.
3. **Stigler (1971)** — capture theory: regulation as an incumbent's instrument.
4. **Pollman & Barry (2017)** — regulatory entrepreneurship: changing the law *as* the business plan (the most legible applied WHY).
5. **Bietti (2020)** — preemption/legitimacy: regulation-advocacy as sophisticated ethics-washing.
6. **Entman (1993)** *or* **Jones & McBeth (2010)** — the framing/NPF toolkit to dissect the narrative.
7. **Coldicutt (2024)** — the anchor: "AI safety" *is* the narrative instrument; the bridge from WHY to HOW.

Together these move students from *motivation* (norms + capture + entrepreneurship) to *legitimation* (ethics-washing) to *instrument* (framing/narrative), which is exactly the WHY-before-HOW arc the lecture needs.
