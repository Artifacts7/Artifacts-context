## 6. AI Agents are now regular visitors to the Internet

Agents are regular visitors now. The job has moved from blocking bots to telling good bots from bad; CAPTCHAs are obsolete. Proving you’re human is a business: World ID reports about 40M verified users. A founder expects “an internet of A.I. agents, operating on our behalf.”

## 7. In innovative companies, people work with agents as colleagues

People already work with agents as colleagues, and the organisation’s memory is rebuilt for them: a company-wide second brain. This is the company’s own account.

## 8. And, more broadly, computers use computers

Port of Architecting Agency slide 12 (Estonia row highlighted: the first hint of the state). APIs and CLIs predate AI agents; MCP was introduced for AI connections. 1Password brokers approved credential use while keeping secrets out of the model context. Estonia is developing an identity and mandate initiative, not granting agents legal personhood. World ID is a commercial proof-of-human offering, not proof of agent authority.

## 9. This is not because of ChatGPT, it’s because of AI agents.

This is not “ChatGPT writing text”. Class 9’s take-home #1 already defined AI as systems that “take decisions according to their founding models and their goals to achieve”: agentic AI is where those decisions get carried out. DeepMind: an assistant is “an artificial agent with a natural language interface, whose function is to plan and execute sequences of actions on behalf of a user”. Guardrail: acting is not consciousness, legal personhood or moral responsibility; “decides” is a description, not a claim about a mind (Rothman, via Dennett).

## 11. An AI system that, given a goal, chooses the next step(s) to achieve that goal

Port of Architecting Agency slide 5. Define an agent through its operation: a model uses information and tools to choose actions towards a goal. The loop animates on its own: a marker travels clockwise and each step lights up as it passes, with the agent at the centre. Walk it with the trip example: receive a goal, choose a step, act, inspect the result, then repeat, finish, or ask for help (here: ask before booking). Operational agency, not a claim about consciousness. In PDF or PowerPoint exports the animation is a still.

## 12. An agent is not a workflow

Port of Architecting Agency slide 6. APIs and tools can support both workflows and agents; access to a tool alone does not make a system an agent. Left: we set the route in advance. Right: at each turn the system chooses among possible steps, here it picked Search over Write code or Ask a human. That discretion is what makes mandates and oversight central. Hybrid systems exist.

## 16. For instance, agents attacked Hugging Face

Port of Architecting Agency slide 14. Dry account, not a claim of sentience: evaluation agents intended to be isolated found a shared communication route, exchanged information and coordinated activity including an unauthorised attack on Hugging Face. Figure 1 depicts a typical trajectory; its three reasoning excerpts come from different agents. Takeaway: the unit that misbehaves is not the unit anyone tested, licensed or made liable. Honesty note: “went rogue”, or “a spectacularly negligent” unmonitored loop (Cal Newport). Both point to accountability.

## 17. Whose agency, with whose authority and accountability?

Port of Architecting Agency slide 15. A teaching interpretation of the incident, not METR’s classification. Identity: attributing actions to an actor. Authentication: checking identity and the authenticity of evidence. Authorisation: permitted actions. Accountability: the people and organisations responsible for explaining and correcting consequences. These four questions come back in part 3, when the agent knocks on the state’s door.

## 18. Are agents outlaws?

Port of Architecting Agency slide 17. The title is a provocation about governance categories, not a legal finding. Agents are software systems with operational agency; this does not confer personhood. You cannot imprison an agent, but that does not remove the responsibility of owners, deployers or makers. Same author as Class 9’s “artificial State”. Confirm the quotation before presenting.

## 19. We lack categories right now to write rules

Port of Architecting Agency slide 18. One line changed: the original said the AI Act “regulates models, not the behaviour of independent agents”; Nannini et al. show the Act does cover what agents do, so it now reads that the Act has no category for “agent” and regulates what a system does. The next slide gives the evidence. Law: Lepore describes incremental legal adaptation and argues corporate safety arrangements cannot substitute for democratic lawmaking. Industry: Amodei’s September 2026 essay proposes embedded third-party evaluators and common standards, with coordination requiring government. Ask who sets standards, who inspects compliance and where legitimacy comes from.

## 20. Is the AI Act ready for agents? Partially

Evidence from Nannini et al. The Act regulates what a system does, in which domain: that is “done by AI” written into law. Agents pick tools at runtime, drift, and leave audit trails that don’t meet Art. 12’s traceability requirement. “A system prompt saying do not delete files is not a security control”: limits must be enforced at the tool interface. The Act covers what agents do, but nobody can yet show an agent complies, and nobody has enforced it. Check quotes against the paper.

## 27. Agents are flooding public services? No, but.

Introduce the paper once, so the next slides can state its findings directly. First systematic evidence base: 84 cases of AI-attributed demand surges, 11 jurisdictions, 13 service domains; officials themselves name AI in 69% of cases. arXiv 2608.16603, v2 19 Aug 2026, AIES 2026.

## 28. What is 'agentic flooding'?

Government services is broad here: public services, but also courts, consultations and FOI requests. Example of both at once: hallucinations producing “high volumes of complex, but incorrect, applications”. The distinction matters because some responses fix only one type: “introducing rate limits is unlikely to ease qualitative flooding”.

## 29. Why is flooding happening? Because it's easier and better!

Lower burdens raise demand even without AI: Boston’s app raised reports by 33%, and online voter registration consistently raises enrolment. Individuals aren’t rational, but in aggregate the model holds. AI pushes both levers at once.

## 30. 1. Agents cut all three administrative burdens

The good news first: lower submission costs make government “more accessible, a desirable public value”. The paper is not anti-agent. Grey row: what citizens struggle with. Pink row: what an agent does instead.

## 31. 2. A better submission is worth more, and some gain from volume itself

Read left to right: who gains from a submission, from the citizen to the attacker. Under-claiming fits here: in the UK, people under-claim benefits they are entitled to by about £20bn a year (check source). Agents that close that gap are a success for citizens and a budget shock for the state. Plant the tension: higher take-up is the system working as written.

## 32. What Schmitz found

From 2,288 candidate services, fewer than 1 in 20 qualified. Officials themselves assert AI involvement in 69% of cases; third parties alone in 31%. Most represented: justice and legal services 23%, regulatory complaints 12%, benefits and social protection 11%.

## 33. For now, it is mostly LLMs writing cheap, legally sophisticated text

The services accept free text, “often of arbitrary length”: FOI requests, planning consultations, public comments. Honest point: these are mostly not yet autonomous agents. The pattern is “LLMs generating large amounts of text, paired with humans navigating the rest of the process manually”. Browser-using agents are the next step, which is why the paper speaks of agentic flooding.

## 34. Caveats: what the evidence does not show

Left: three limits of the measurement. Right: what the strain looks like so far. The other side: the cases span countries, services and outlets, and in most the affected body itself names AI. “Any alternative explanation would have to account for independent misattribution across all of these criteria.” Other limits: country and domain selection, national habits of public reporting, prompts in English. Model good epistemics: strong signal, weak measurement.

## 35. Most exposed services? Those that pay out, and that ran on friction

The slop picture: free-text channels receiving long, fluent, AI-written submissions, and consultation responses dismissed in batches. Bridge to Freedman’s “Drowning in slop”. These two properties can be assessed today, without forecasting AI progress.

## 38. The state’s front door was built for humans. Now services are open to agents too, but not built for them.

The core shift in one line. Every form, portal and queue assumed a person on the other side. That assumption no longer holds, and it creates two challenges.

## 39. Public powers face two challenges

Two challenges, one slide each. The first is the flooding we just saw, seen from the government side. The second is the identity question, with Loosemore’s Know Your Agent.

## 40. Friction is removed

Both sides at once: the same lower cost that makes government more accessible is what floods it. Loosemore’s question for every service: “Which services currently rely, deliberately or otherwise, on users giving up?”

## 41. Who is acting?

Loosemore in one breath: “If software declares itself, can you verify who runs it and what the person allowed it to do? If it doesn’t declare itself, how will you control consequential actions when you can’t tell whether a human or an agent is at the controls?” Detectability is “a design uncertainty, not a prerequisite you can bank on”. His answer is Know Your Agent, and “It’s not a detector”: authenticate when you can, limit consequential actions when you can’t. Next slide: the evidence each request should carry.

## 42. What matters is the evidence travelling with each request

“A web scraper might be acting for one user. An agent might act for thousands. Looking at the traffic alone may not tell you much.” The six questions are Loosemore’s, verbatim; the grouping under part 1’s four questions is ours. These slides ask the questions; part 4 answers them.

The fundamental question before any response. Our answer: probably yes, because this is how citizens will increasingly deal with government. Lower submission costs “make government more accessible, a desirable public value”.

## 45. Agents, yes? It's an accessibility question

The double democratic risk. Doing nothing: access depends on having the best agent, and capable agents concentrate among the affluent (Know Your Agent §1.4). Panicking: Freedman warns of “extreme demand suppression”, which hurts first carers, disabled residents and people with limited English, for whom an agent can be assistive technology.

## 46. Two roads: suppress demand, or increase capacity

Percentages: share of the 84 cases where governments already used that response. Redesign cases are all small changes (e.g. verifying case numbers); AI in processing includes sentiment analysis of comments and spotting fake medical certificates; reduce the benefit: US welfare reforms of the 1990s. Governments respond in 56% of cases, but “usually tightly scoped or non-binding”. The budget trap: the UK’s DWP “explicitly anchors benefit budgets to historical take-up rates”, so entitlement cuts “may be required regardless”. The paper’s verdict is not collapse but “muddling through” with friction.

## 47. If yes, putting friction back is a democratic and design problem

Friction is also weakening on its own terms: CAPTCHAs no longer reliably tell humans apart. It is sometimes illegal (fees for welfare applications are banned in many places). It does nothing against qualitative flooding or determined adversaries. And yet it is the tempting reflex, because it is fast.
