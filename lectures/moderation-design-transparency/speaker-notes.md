## 4. “Content moderation is the commodity platforms offer”

Content moderation is often seen as a back-office process. It isn't only that. In Gillespie's words, it is the commodity platforms offer: a fundamental aspect of their services and of their place in public discourse; the way platforms don't just mediate public discourse but constitute it, shaping the norms and values of online communities; a delicate balance of social and political values; and an enormous part of running a platform in people, time and costs. And so moderation is a prism for understanding what platforms are and their public role, and why platforms are not just intermediaries.

## 5. Platforms govern the spaces they built. Moderation is how.

Platforms govern the spaces they built, within the limits of the law, and moderation is how. Moderation is the design of what can and can't be said online and, importantly, of what happens if you break the rules. But what does it mean for a platform to govern? Platforms often present themselves as modern public squares, or that status is conferred by users, politicians and institutions who rely on them for official communication. Musk, March 2022: Twitter as the "de facto public town square".

## 6. Calling platforms “public squares” may be misleading

But calling platforms public squares is misleading. They are run and owned by private companies that operate to maximise profit. So content recommendation and moderation are driven primarily by business priorities rather than commitments to neutrality or open debate. Nevertheless, they do play a public role. Let's look at a real story.

## 7. Let’s look at a real story

Let's look at a real story: how Meta changed its approach to political content, and to moderation, between 2021 and 2025.

## 8. 2021: Meta starts reducing political content

February 2021: after Zuckerberg says people don't want political content taking over their feed, Meta temporarily reduces the distribution of political content for a small share of users in Canada, Brazil and Indonesia, then the US. August 2021: positive results, so the tests expand to Costa Rica, Sweden, Spain and Ireland.

## 9. 2022: the tests go global

May 2022: to reduce political content, Meta puts less weight on comments and shares when ranking it, and expands the tests globally. July 2022: the tests conclude that de-emphasising shares and comments works, and the changes are implemented globally. Note the lever: not removal, but ranking signals.

## 10. 2024: users get a political content setting

September 2024: Meta rolls out a Political Content setting. Changing from the default can increase political content from accounts people follow, and recommendations from accounts they don't. The decision shifts from the platform to the user, but the default stays with the platform.

## 11. 2025: “More speech and fewer mistakes”

January 2025, "More Speech and Fewer Mistakes". Three moves. Automated systems, which Meta says made too many mistakes, now focus on illegal and high-severity violations: terrorism, child sexual exploitation, drugs, fraud and scams. For less severe violations, Meta waits for someone to report an issue before acting. And political content, reduced since 2021 in what Meta calls a "pretty blunt approach", is phased back in with a personalised approach. All framed as Meta's responsibility to protect people's ability to make their voices heard.

## 12. Five years of moderation AND design decisions

To recap the story in one view. 2021 to 2022, the direction is less political content: tests in Canada, Brazil and Indonesia, then the US; expansion to Costa Rica, Sweden, Spain and Ireland; less weight on comments and shares; global roll-out. 2024, user choice: a setting to opt into more political content. January 2025, more speech: "More Speech and Fewer Mistakes" narrows automated enforcement to severe harms and waits for user reports on the rest, while political content comes back.

## 13. A technical and design decision

Let's go deeper on January 2025. Zuckerberg put it plainly: we're going to catch less bad stuff, but we'll also reduce the number of innocent people whose posts get taken down. That's not only a political statement. It's a technical and design decision.

## 14. A technical and design decision

In technical terms, Meta moved from recall to precision. Recall asks: of all violating content, how much did we catch? Precision asks: of what we took down, how much was actually violating? Before, the goal was to catch everything, at the risk of errors: safe content gets cut and freedom of speech is restricted. Those are false positives. After, the net narrows to severe harms only, knowing other harmful content may go uncaught. Those are false negatives. It's not a technical tweak; it's a choice of which error you'd rather make.

## 15. A technical and design decision

Picture a fisherman looking for fish with golden scales. The precision fisherman casts carefully and catches only 20 fish, all golden; they might miss a few, but every catch counts. The recall fisherman throws a wide net and catches 100 fish so no golden one escapes, but has to sort through a lot of plain fish. Meta wants to be the precision fisherman: it would rather remove 20 harmful pieces of content with near certainty than remove 100 and risk taking down innocent posts by mistake.

## 22. The DSA doesn’t set out any fundamentally new content rules. Instead, it focuses mostly on buckets 2 and 3

In terms of how it approaches content moderation - it doesn't set any new fundamental content rules:
- Arts. 4–6: Liability regime carried over from E-Commerce Directive
- Art. 3: Illegal content not defined in the DSA

Instead it focuses a lot more on "buckets 2 and 3"

## 23. The DSA focuses more on how platforms moderate…

For bucket 2, content moderation design, it sets some answers to many of the questions we saw before in that slide.

Some are very specific
1) Who can trigger moderation: Government orders; User reports of illegal content; TFs -> Checklist
2) How do users find out, and can they push back

Others are more 'guidance'
2) Who or what decides: Doesn't answer but asks for transparency
3) What harms do we prioritise and how do we define them: Acknowledges platforms take action based on their terms of service (their internal community guidelines) and that these guidelines may prohibit content that is harmful but legal. Beyond prohibiting illegal content, the DSA doesn't prescribe what 'harmful but legal content' needs to be in those community gudelines - but it asks transparency and proportionality

## 24. … and on building mechanisms to make platforms externally accountable for the results of their moderation systems

It also has quite a bit on bucket 3, accountability: Less about prescribing how platforms set up their content moderation systems and more about creating transparency, engaging platforms' external responsibility beyond e.g. a lawsuit, for the results of those systems

For example: periodic tranpsarency reports on the results of their content moderation
Risk assessments on how they may create

...

Accountability not just against regulators - much broader audience

And this accountability regime was really one of the things that set the DSA apart when it first came to be

## 27. Transparency reports

Let's look at a real story: how Meta changed its approach to political content, and to moderation, between 2021 and 2025.

## 28. What is a transparency report?

So what is a transparency report? Fundamentally, it's an Excel file. It tracks, records and logs every moderation decision the platform takes, on reports coming from authorities, users, trusted flaggers and the platform's own detection; the tools used to moderate and how much is automated; how many human moderators do the work; and appeals, disputes and repeat offenders. Under the DSA, Article 15 requires every intermediary service to publish one at least once a year; Article 24 adds information for online platforms, such as out-of-court disputes, suspensions and average monthly active users; Article 42 requires very large platforms and search engines to report every six months, including on their moderators and the languages they cover. Since 1 July 2025 everyone uses the same template, set by Implementing Regulation 2024/2835, as a CSV or XLSX file.

## 29. It asks for data about content moderation decisions (1/2)

Each section of the template asks a design question. Who can trigger moderation: orders from authorities, user notices, trusted flaggers, and the platform's own initiative. What harms do we prioritise: counts per EU category. Law or our own rules: actions on the basis of law versus terms and conditions. What do we do about it: the type of restriction. What AI do we use: automated versus human decisions. And how good is that AI: accuracy, precision and recall, which takes us straight back to Meta.

## 30. It asks for data about content moderation decisions (2/2)

The second half: how many people review, and in which languages; how fast the platform acts; whether users can push back through complaints and out-of-court dispute settlement, and how often decisions are reversed; what it takes to ban someone, including people who abuse the reporting system; and how the platform explains all of this in its own words. Back to Meta: if the January 2025 shift happened as described, we'd expect fewer own-initiative actions and more notice-based ones in its DSA reports. ## 34. Transparency reports give us a record of what moderation systems do, but numbers alone can't tell if a service is 'doing well'

Transparency reports give a record of what moderator systems do but volumes alone don't answer whether a service is "doing well".

For example, say you compare a platform's volume of removal orders this year vs the last - removals have increased 2x. Is this good or bad news?

Risk assessments provide one potential approach to answering that questionfor. I’ll take this in three steps: first the general logic of a risk assessment, then what the DSA actually requires, and then we’ll walk through one real example together

## 35. Risk assessments widen the lens: how could this service cause harm, what controls are in place, how well do they work?

Step one, the general logic. This isn’t DSA-specific: it’s how online safety risk assessments generally work

Risk assessments change the question. Instead of ‘what/how much did you remove?’, they ask ‘what could go wrong on this service, and is what you do about it enough?’

Evidence band. “Moderation data, like the transparency report figures from deep dive 1, becomes one input among several. User research, external reports and incidents count too.”

Box 1, how harm could occur. “Harm comes from the combination of features, users, content and behaviour, not just from content.” One sentence is enough here.
Box 2, controls.  Any safety measures - content moderation systems, but also privacy defaults, age assurance and user tools.”
Bottom row. “The logic is simple. How serious is the risk before any controls? How well do the controls work? What’s left, and is that acceptable?”

## 36. Under the DSA, the largest platforms assess ‘systemic risks’ in their service and adopt mitigation strategies

Onto the legal requirement. The DSA takes this logic and makes it mandatory for the largest platforms.

- Who and when. “This applies only to the largest platforms and search engines, those with 45 million or more EU users. They must assess at least yearly, and before launching features likely to have a critical impact.”
- Art. 35 picks up where the previous slide ended: if residual risk isn’t acceptable, platforms have to adopt further measures. Then, the results of that risk assessment + mitigation effort are made public.”
- What to assess. “Look at how broad these categories are. Illegal content is only one of them. Most concern harms that aren’t necessarily illegal, like well-being, civic discourse and minors.” “The ‘influencing factors’ are the DSA's way of referring to certain design and feature elements from the previous slide, which need to be considered in the risk assessment.

Notice what the law doesn’t tell you: what 'illegal content' or ‘protection of minors’ actually means, or the step by step for how to assess it. That’s mostly left to platforms (although the Commission will sometimes provide guidance). So step three: let’s see what that looks like in practice
